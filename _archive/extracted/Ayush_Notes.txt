Introduction to Computer Networks 
The session began with a broad overview of computer networks and their significance in 
modern communication systems. The instructor emphasized the importance of networks in 
connecting various computing devices over both short and long distances to facilitate 
communication and data exchange. 
Purpose of Networks 
●​
Networks are essential for communication across distances, enabling the sharing of 
resources and information. 
●​
They are critical in both professional settings and personal life, affecting everything from 
enterprise data centers to consumer internet experiences. 
Components of a Computer Network 
●​
Nodes and Links: Nodes (also known as devices) are connected via links (the physical or 
wireless means that transmit data) forming the foundational structure of any network. 
●​
Types of Nodes: Common types include computers, routers, switches, and hubs. 
Types of Delays in Networks 
Delays are inevitable in network communications, but understanding them can lead to more 
efficient network design and usage: 
●​
Processing Delay: Time taken to process the packet header at each node. 
●​
Queuing Delay: Time a packet spends waiting in line at a particular node. 
●​
Transmission Delay: Time required to push all of the packet's bits into the link. 
●​
Propagation Delay: Time it takes for a signal to propagate through the medium to the next 
node. 
Communication Types 
●​
Circuit Switching: A dedicated communication path is established between two nodes for the 
duration of the session, akin to traditional telephone systems. 
●​
Packet Switching: Data is broken into packets that travel independently through the network. 
More efficient and widely used for data networks like the internet. 
Network Infrastructure Components 
●​
Network Edge: Where the end devices such as personal computers are located. 
●​
Network Core: The central part of the network infrastructure that connects devices and 
networks, effectively transporting data between nodes.

Basics of Internet 
●​
LAN (Local Area Network): Operates within a localized area, such as a home or office, 
characterized by high data rates and minimal latency issues. 
●​
WAN (Wide Area Network): Covers larger geographical areas such as cities and nations, 
usually operated by telecommunications companies. 
Key Terminologies 
●​
IP Address: A unique address that identifies a device on a network, essential for directing 
data to the correct destination. 
●​
Latency: Refers to the time delay from when a data packet is sent to when it is received. It is 
influenced by both distance and the speed of the data pathway. 
Reliability in Networks 
To ensure reliability, protocols are implemented which manage data transmission, error 
checking, and retransmission strategies. These protocols are crucial in mitigating disruptions 
and maintaining a consistent level of service across networks. 
Real-World Analogies 
An analogy used during the lecture compares network communication to postal services, where 
data packets are like letters that are routed through various post offices (nodes) before reaching 
their destination.

Maximum Transmission Unit (MTU) and Fragmentation 
MTU 
●​
MTU (Maximum Transmission Unit) is the maximum size of a payload that can be carried in 
a single frame on a given link type【6:8†transcript.txt】. 
●​
For Ethernet, the standard MTU is 1500 bytes【6:0†transcript.txt】. 
IP Fragmentation 
●​
IP Fragmentation occurs when a packet is too large to be sent in a single frame according to 
the MTU. 
●​
A 2000-byte IP packet would be broken into two fragments if the MTU is 1500 bytes【
6:0†transcript.txt】. 
●​
Fragmentation increases the number of packets, leading to more processing【
6:8†transcript.txt】. 
●​
TCP protocol often avoids fragmentation by determining the Maximum Segment Size (MSS) 
to divide data into smaller units【6:0†transcript.txt】.
